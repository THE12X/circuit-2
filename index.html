SECTION 1.1 • PAGES 3–10

Function and Components of Circuits

Definition: Circuit

A closed path or loop around which electric current flows.

Actual Circuit

A combination of physical electrical equipment or devices connected in a certain way for a specific practical purpose.

Circuit Model

An idealized representation composed of ideal circuit elements ($R, L, C, U_S, I_S$) used for mathematical analysis of an actual circuit.

Classification Primary Function & Signal/Energy Flow Characteristics & Members Engineering Focus
1. Power Circuit Generation, transmission, and conversion of electrical energy.
Generator → Step-up Transformer → Transmission Line → Step-down Transformer → Loads (Lights [light energy], Heater [heat energy], Motor [mechanical energy]) 		Characteristics: Voltage, current, power.
Members: Lighting systems, power distribution grids. 		Efficiency ($\eta$) & safe energy delivery
2. Signal Circuit Transmission and processing of information/signals.
Source / Microphone → Amplifier → Other Signal Processing Unit → Loudspeaker (Electrical signal → Sound signal) 		Characteristics: Voltage, current, power.
Members: CATV, telephone, computer network, Wi-Fi. 		Effect (Ensure high signal transmission quality / fidelity)

3 PARTS Basic Circuit Components

  • 1. Sources (Power Supply): Device that supplies electrical energy or signals (e.g., battery, generator). Note: Actual output power depends on the load.
  • 2. Load: Device that converts electrical energy into non-electrical energy (e.g., bulb, motor, heater).
  • 3. Intermediate Link: Equipment for transmission, control, and protection of electrical energy (conductors/wires, switches, fuses, relays).
Example — Electric Torch: Source = Battery; Load = Small Bulb; Intermediate Link = Switch & Spring.

REVIEW Q&A Key Concept Rules & Terminology

  • What does "Load Increase" mean? It means the load consumes more electric power, i.e., the load current $I$ increases (typically by adding parallel loads, reducing equivalent load resistance).
  • Classification Q&A: A simple temperature-measuring circuit (thermocouple + mA meter) is a Signal Circuit; a battery + bulb lighting circuit is a Power Circuit.
  • Basic Terms: • DC (Direct Current): Currents & voltages are constant with time.
    • AC (Alternating Current): Currents & voltages alternate with time.
    • Sinusoidal AC: Currents & voltages alternate in sinusoidal form.
    • Two-terminal Network: A network with only two wires connected to external networks.
    • Passive vs. Active Network: Passive contains only passive elements ($R, L, C$) with no independent internal sources; Active contains internal energy sources.
SECTION 1.2 • PAGES 11–18

Basic Electrical Quantities ($I, V, U, E, P, W$)

1.2.1 CURRENT ($I$)

Electric charge flowing past a given point per unit of time. Unit: Ampere ($\text{A}$).

$$I = \frac{Q}{t} \quad \left(1\text{ A} = \frac{1\text{ C}}{1\text{ s}}\right)$$
1.2.2 ELECTRIC POTENTIAL ($V$)

Work done by the electric field to move a unit positive charge from a node to the reference node. Unit: Volt ($\text{V}$).

Rule: Potential is relative — meaningful ONLY when a reference node ($0\text{V}$) is specified.
1.2.3 VOLTAGE ($U$ or $V$)

Energy required to move a unit charge between two points (potential difference). $U_S$: source voltage; $U_L$: load voltage. Unit: Volt ($\text{V}$).

$$U_{ab} = V_a - V_b = \frac{W_{ab}}{Q}$$
1.2.4 ELECTROMOTIVE FORCE ($E$)

Work done by non-electric forces (chemical, mechanical) to move a unit charge through the source from $-$ to $+$.

Distinction: Same unit ($\text{V}$) as voltage, but $E$ acts inside the source, while $U$ exists between two points in the external circuit.
1.2.5 ELECTRIC POWER ($P$)

Time rate of providing or absorbing electrical energy. Unit: Watt ($\text{W}$).

$$P_S = U_S I \quad \text{(Source)}, \quad P_L = U_L I \quad \text{(Load)}$$
1.2.6 ELECTRIC ENERGY ($W$)

Total work done / energy consumed over time $t$. SI Unit: Joule ($\text{J}$); Engineering unit: $\text{kW}\cdot\text{h}$.

$$W = P \cdot t, \quad 1\text{ kW}\cdot\text{h} = 3.6 \times 10^6\text{ J}$$
Worked Examples — Section 1.2
Ex 1.2A: Potential vs. Voltage ($10\text{V}$, $R_1=2\Omega$, $R_2=2\Omega$)
  • • Voltage: $I = 10/(2+2)=2.5\text{A} \implies U_{ab}=5\text{V}, U_{bc}=5\text{V}$ (always fixed).
  • • If $c$ is reference ($V_c=0\text{V}$): $V_a = 10\text{V}, V_b = 5\text{V}$.
  • • If no reference node is given: Potentials $V_a, V_b, V_c$ cannot be determined.
  • • If $a$ is reference ($V_a=0\text{V}$): $V_b = U_{ba} = -5\text{V}, V_c = -10\text{V}$.
Ex 1.2B: Bulb Power Calculation

A light bulb operates at voltage $U = 6\text{V}$ and draws current $I = 1.6\text{A}$. Find the power absorbed.

$$P = U \times I = 6\text{V} \times 1.6\text{A} = 9.6\text{W}$$
Ex 1.2C: Electric Energy Consumption

A small indicator bulb draws $I = 10\text{mA}$ ($0.01\text{A}$) at $U = 2\text{V}$ for $t = 30\text{ minutes}$ ($1800\text{s}$). Find energy $W$.

$$P = 2\text{V} \times 0.01\text{A} = 0.02\text{W}$$ $$W = P \cdot t = 0.02 \times 1800 = 36\text{ J} = 10^{-5}\text{ kW}\cdot\text{h}$$
SECTION 1.3 • PAGES 19–22

Circuit Conditions: Closed, Open, and Short Circuits

1.3.1 CLOSED CIRCUIT (LOADED) NORMAL

Switch closed; continuous path without interruption; energy transmission and conversion occur.

$I = \dfrac{U_S}{R_0 + R}$
$U = IR = U_S - IR_0$
$P_S = U_S I, \quad P = UI$
$\Delta P = I^2 R_0, \quad P = P_S - \Delta P$
1.3.2 OPEN CIRCUIT (NO LOAD) $R \to \infty$

Part of the circuit is opened (switch open or broken wire); no current flows.

$I = 0 \quad \text{(always zero)}$
$U = U_{OC} = U_S$
$P = 0 \quad \text{(no power delivered)}$
1.3.3 SHORT CIRCUIT FAULT ($R=0$)

Direct connection between two points not supposed to be directly connected. Dangerous high current!

$U = 0 \quad \text{(always zero)}$
$I = I_{SC} = \dfrac{U_S}{R_0} \quad \text{(max current)}$
$P_{\text{load}} = 0 \quad (\text{all } P_S \text{ lost in } R_0)$
WORKED EXAMPLE 1.3 (PAGE 22)

Problem: A practical voltage source has open-circuit EMF $U_S = 125\text{V}$. When connected to a load, terminal voltage drops to $U = 115\text{V}$ and load current is $I = 4\text{A}$. Find generated power $P_S$, load power $P$, and internal resistance $R_0$.

$P_S = U_S I = 125 \times 4 = \mathbf{500\text{W}}$
$P = U I = 115 \times 4 = \mathbf{460\text{W}}$
$R_0 = \dfrac{U_S - U}{I} = \dfrac{125 - 115}{4} = \mathbf{2.5\Omega}$

SECTION 1.4 • PAGES 23–25

Reference Point and Reference Direction

1.4.1 REFERENCE POINT (GROUND = $0\text{V}$)
  • • Electric potential is relative and meaningful only when a reference point is given.
  • • The reference point is assigned potential $0\text{V}$ and called ground ($\perp\!\!\!\perp$).
  • • Voltage between any two nodes $a, b$ equals their potential difference: $U_{ab} = V_a - V_b$.
Worked Ex 1.4A: $10\text{V}$ source in series with $4\Omega$ ($a\to b$), $3\Omega$ ($b\to c$), $3\Omega$ ($c\to d$). Without ground, $V_a, V_b, V_c$ cannot be determined. With $d$ grounded ($V_d=0\text{V}$): $I = 10/(4+3+3) = 1\text{A} \implies V_a = 10\text{V}, V_b = 6\text{V}, V_c = 3\text{V}$.
Worked Ex 1.4B: Two equal resistors $R$ in series between $a$ ($V_a = 12\text{V}$) and $b$ ($V_b = -10\text{V}$), midpoint $c$: $V_c = \dfrac{1}{2}(V_a - V_b) + V_b = \dfrac{1}{2}(12 - (-10)) - 10 = \mathbf{1\text{V}}$
1.4.2 REFERENCE DIRECTION & SIGN INTERPRETATION
  • • Assumed Direction: Arbitrarily assigned before analysis so algebraic equations can be written.
  • • Sign Rule: If calculated value is positive ($>0$), actual direction matches reference direction; if negative ($<0$), actual direction is opposite to reference direction.
  • • 3 Voltage Representations: (1) Arrow (from $+$ to $-$), (2) Polarity signs ($+$ and $-$), (3) Double subscripts ($U_{AB}$ = potential drop from $A$ to $B$).
Associated Reference Direction Rule: When voltage and current have the same reference direction ($I$ enters $+$ terminal of $U$), Ohm's law for a resistor is $U = RI$. If opposite, $U = -RI$.
SECTION 1.5 • PAGES 26–30

Ideal Circuit Elements & Power Absorbed vs. Supplied Rule

1.5.1 IDEAL PASSIVE ELEMENTS (ASSOCIATED REF. DIR.)
Resistor ($R$): Dissipates electrical energy $U = I R$
Inductor ($L$): Stores magnetic energy $u = -e = L \dfrac{di}{dt}$
Capacitor ($C$): Stores electric energy $i = \dfrac{dq}{dt} = C \dfrac{du}{dt}$
1.5.2 IDEAL ACTIVE ELEMENTS
Ideal Voltage Source ($U_S$): Terminal voltage $U = U_S = \text{const.}$ (independent of current); current $I$ is determined by external circuit.
Ex: $U_S=10\text{V}$ with $R_1=2\Omega \implies I=5\text{A}$; with $2\Omega \parallel 2\Omega \implies I=10\text{A}$.
Ideal Current Source ($I_S$): Output current $I = I_S = \text{const.}$ (independent of terminal voltage); terminal voltage $U$ is determined by external circuit.
Ex: $I_S=1\text{A}$ with $R=1\Omega \implies U=1\text{V}$; with $R=10\Omega \implies U=10\text{V}$.
1.5.3 CRITICAL RULE: POWER ABSORBED VS. POWER SUPPLIED (PAGES 27–29)
Not all sources supply power!
Same Direction (Associated): When $U$ and $I$ have the same reference direction ($I$ enters $+$ terminal), the element absorbs power ($P = UI > 0$ absorbed).
Opposite Direction (Non-Associated): When $U$ and $I$ have opposite reference directions ($I$ leaves $+$ terminal), the element supplies power ($P = UI > 0$ supplied).

SECTION 1.6 • PAGES 31–38

Practical Sources & Equivalent Source Transformation (ST)

1.6.1 & 1.6.2 PRACTICAL SOURCE MODELS
Practical Voltage Source: Ideal voltage source $U_S$ in series with $R_0$:
$U = U_S - I R_0 \neq \text{const.}$
Practical Current Source: Ideal current source $I_S$ in parallel with $R_0$:
$I = I_S - \dfrac{U}{R_0} \neq \text{const.}$
1.6.3 Equivalence Formulas & Current Divider:
• $V \to I$: $I_S = \dfrac{U_S}{R}, \quad R' = R$ (series $\to$ parallel)
• $I \to V$: $U_S = I_S R, \quad R' = R$ (parallel $\to$ series)
• Current Divider: $I_1 = \dfrac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} I, \quad I_2 = \dfrac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2} I$
ALL 5 SOURCE TRANSFORMATION Q&A RULES & REDUNDANCY LAWS
  1. What is ST? Replacing a practical voltage source by an equivalent practical current source (or vice versa).
  2. Purpose: Simplify multi-source circuit calculations.
  3. 3 Conditions / Restrictions:
    • Only valid for practical sources, NOT ideal sources ($R=0$ or $R=\infty$).
    • Equivalent only for the external circuit, NOT for the inside of the source itself.
    • Keep reference direction consistent: The current source arrow points toward the $+$ polarity of the equivalent voltage source.
  4. Special Redundancy Rules (Page 35):
    • Any branch in parallel with an ideal voltage source has no influence on the external circuit (can be removed for external analysis).
    • Any branch in series with an ideal current source has no influence on the external circuit (can be shorted/removed for external analysis).

1.7.1 KIRCHHOFF'S CURRENT LAW (KCL)

The algebraic sum of currents entering a node (or closed boundary) is zero at any instant:

$$\sum i = 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \sum I_{\text{in}} = \sum I_{\text{out}}$$
  • • Generalized KCL (Closed Surface): Applies to any closed boundary (e.g., BJT transistor $I_E = I_B + I_C$, $\Delta$-network $I_1 + I_2 = I_3$, or 4-node subcircuit $I_1+I_2+I_3+I_4 = 0$).
  • • Node A Example: $I_1, I_3$ entering; $I_2, I_4, I_5$ leaving $\implies I_1 + I_3 - I_2 - I_4 - I_5 = 0$.
1.7.2 KIRCHHOFF'S VOLTAGE LAW (KVL)

The algebraic sum of all voltages around any closed loop (or imaginary open-terminal loop) is zero:

$$\sum u = 0 \quad (\text{or } \sum U = 0)$$
  • • Sign Rule: Choose clockwise/CCW traversal. Voltage drops ($+$ to $-$) in traversal direction take $+$, rises ($-$ to $+$) take $-$.
  • • Ex E1 & E2: $U_1 - U_2 + U_3 - U_4 = 0$; Two-source loop: $U_{S2} + R_2 I_2 + R_1 I_1 - U_{S1} = 0$.
  • • Ex E3 (Imaginary/Open-Terminal Loop): If $I$ flows down through $R$: $U_S + RI - U = 0 \implies U = U_S + RI$.
    If $I$ flows up through $R$: $U_S - RI - U = 0 \implies U = U_S - RI$.

4 MANDATORY STEPS FOR BRANCH CURRENT ANALYSIS
  1. Directions: Assign reference directions to all $B$ branch currents and choose loop traversal directions.
  2. KCL Equations: Write KCL equations for the $(N - 1)$ independent nodes.
  3. KVL Equations: Write KVL equations for the $M = B - (N - 1)$ independent loops/meshes.
  4. Solve: Solve the linear system of $B$ independent equations for all $B$ branch currents.

SECTION 1.9 • PAGES 61–67

Superposition Theorem

THEOREM STATEMENT & SOURCE DEACTIVATION RULES

In any linear circuit, the voltage across or current through any element equals the algebraic sum of the voltages or currents caused by each independent source acting alone.

How to Turn Off ("Kill") Other Independent Sources:
• Ideal Voltage Source ($U_S=0$): Replace by a short circuit.
• Ideal Current Source ($I_S=0$): Replace by an open circuit.
• Practical Sources: Retain their internal resistances $R_0$.
3 Steps: (1) Keep one source active, deactivate others, solve partial response $I'$ or $U'$; (2) Repeat for each independent source; (3) Sum algebraically respecting reference signs ($I = I' + I'' + \dots$).
4 CRITICAL NOTICES & EXAM TRAPS (PAGE 64)
  1. Linearity Only: Valid strictly for linear circuits (not diodes/transistors).
  2. NEVER Apply to Power ($P \neq P' + P''$): Power is nonlinear ($P = I^2 R$ or $U^2 / R$). Always find total $I$ or $U$ first, then compute $P = I^2 R$.
  3. Reference Direction / Sign: Assign consistent reference directions; subtract partial terms that oppose the reference direction.
  4. Source Grouping: Sub-circuits can group multiple active sources together if convenient.
WORKED EXAMPLES E1–E5 (PAGES 65–67)
Ex E1 & E2 ($20\text{V}$ & $4\text{A}$ with $10\Omega$ resistors)
• E1: $I_1 = I_1' + I_1''$, $I_2 = I_2' + I_2''$, $I_3 = I_3' + I_3''$.
• E2: $20\text{V}$ alone $\implies I' = 2\text{A}$; $4\text{A}$ alone $\implies I'' = -1\text{A} \implies I = 2 + (-1) = \mathbf{1\text{A}}$.
Ex E3 ($7\text{A}$ source & $35\text{V}$ source)
• $7\text{A}$ source acting alone $\implies I' = 3\text{A}$.
• $35\text{V}$ source acting alone $\implies I'' = 5\text{A}$.
• Total current $I = 3 + 5 = \mathbf{8\text{A}}$.
Ex E4 & E5 (Page 67)
• E4 ($3\text{A}, 5\text{A}, 10\text{V}$): Deactivate each source in turn and sum algebraically.
• E5 ($18\text{V}+1\Omega$, middle $4\Omega$, $9\text{V}+1\Omega$): $18\text{V}$ alone $\implies I' = 2\text{A}$; $9\text{V}$ alone $\implies I'' = 1\text{A} \implies I = \mathbf{3\text{A}}$.
SECTION 1.10 • PAGES 68–80

Equivalent Source Theorem: Thévenin's & Norton's Theorems

1.10.2 THÉVENIN'S THEOREM

Any active linear two-terminal network can be replaced by an equivalent voltage source $U_{eS} = U_{OC}$ in series with an equivalent resistance $R_0$.

1. Find $U_{eS} = U_{OC}$: Disconnect the load at terminals $a, b$ and compute the open-circuit terminal voltage $U_{ab}$.
2. Find $R_0$: • Method 1 (Independent sources only): Turn off all internal independent sources (short $U_S$, open $I_S$) and find equivalent resistance looking into $a, b$. • Method 2 (General): $R_0 = \dfrac{U_{OC}}{I_{SC}}$.
Worked Ex (pp. 72–76, Load $R_L=8\Omega$): $R_0 = 2+2+2 = 6\Omega$; KVL $U_{ab}+5-15-4=0 \implies U_{ab}=14\text{V}$; Load current $I = \dfrac{14}{6+8} = \mathbf{1\text{A}}$.
1.10.3 NORTON'S THEOREM

Any active linear two-terminal circuit can be replaced by an equivalent current source $I_{eS} = I_{SC}$ in parallel with the equivalent resistance $R_0$.

1. Find $I_{eS} = I_{SC}$: Short-circuit terminals $a, b$ and compute the short-circuit current $I_{SC}$ flowing from $a$ to $b$.
2. Find $R_0$: Same $R_0$ as Thévenin ($R_0 = U_{OC}/I_{SC}$).
Worked Ex (pp. 77–80, Load $R_L=8\Omega$): $R_0 = 6\Omega$; $I_{eS} = \dfrac{U_{ab}}{R_0} = \dfrac{14}{6}\text{A} = \dfrac{7}{3}\text{A}$; Current divider gives $I = \dfrac{6}{6+8} \times \dfrac{14}{6} = \mathbf{1\text{A}}$.

SECTION 2.1 • PAGES 82–90

Basic Concepts: Steady State, Transient Process & Energy Storage

2.1.1 STEADY STATE, TRANSIENT & SWITCHING
  • • Steady State: When circuit structure and parameters are fixed, all currents and voltages remain unchanged (invariable).
  • • Transient Process: The transition period from one steady state to a new steady state following a disturbance.
  • • Switching: Any sudden change in circuit structure (opening/closing switch), circuit parameters, or power supply.
Three-Lamp Demonstration (Switch $S$ closed at $t=0$):
• Lamp $L_1$ (Resistor $R$ branch): Lights up immediately and brightness stays constant (no transient).
• Lamp $L_2$ (Inductor $L$ branch): Changes gradually from dark to bright, then glows stably.
• Lamp $L_3$ (Capacitor $C$ branch): Suddenly flashes brightly, gradually dims, and finally goes out (open circuit in DC steady state).
2.1.2 & 2.1.3 ENERGY STORAGE & 2 MANDATORY CONDITIONS
Capacitor Electric Energy
$$W_e = \frac{1}{2} C u_C^2$$
$u_C$ cannot jump abruptly
Inductor Magnetic Energy
$$W_m = \frac{1}{2} L i_L^2$$
$i_L$ cannot jump abruptly
Two Required Conditions for a Transient Process to Occur:
1. External Cause: Switching occurs (change in circuit structure, parameters, or source).
2. Internal Cause: Presence of energy-storage elements ($C$ or $L$), since stored energy cannot change instantaneously (otherwise $P = dW/dt = \infty$).
2.1.4 Applications & Hazards: Used in RC time-delay circuits, high-voltage pulse/lightning generators, and fluorescent lamp starters (ballast $L$ + starter). Must protect equipment against transient over-voltage and over-current spikes.

Switching Law (Commutation Law)

TIME INSTANT NOTATION
• $t = 0$: Exact instant of switching.
• $t = 0_-$: Moment immediately before switching (evaluated from pre-switching DC steady state: $C$ = open circuit, $L$ = short circuit).
• $t = 0_+$: Moment immediately after switching.
LAW 1: CAPACITOR SWITCHING LAW

Voltage across a capacitor cannot change abruptly at the moment of switching:

$$u_C(0_+) = u_C(0_-)$$
LAW 2: INDUCTOR SWITCHING LAW

Current through an inductor cannot change abruptly at the moment of switching:

$$i_L(0_+) = i_L(0_-)$$
CRITICAL NOTICE (PAGE 94): "Not to change abruptly" does NOT mean "not to change at all" — it means $u_C(t)$ and $i_L(t)$ vary continuously across $t=0$. By contrast, non-state variables such as capacitor current $i_C$, inductor voltage $u_L$, and resistor voltages/currents ($u_R, i_R$) CAN jump abruptly at $t = 0_+$!
Worked Ex 2.2-1 (Page 95)

DC steady-state circuit ($6\text{V}$ source, three $2\Omega$ resistors, $C$, $L$):

In steady state, $C$ is open ($I_3 = 0\text{A}$), $L$ is shorted ($U_L = 0\text{V}$), total $I = 2\text{A}$, $I_1 = 1\text{A}$, $I_2 = 1\text{A}$, $U_C = 2\text{V}$.
Worked Ex 2.2-2 (Page 96)

Switch thrown from position A to B at $t=0$:

Only $u_C$ and $i_L$ remain unchanged at $t=0_+$ ($u_C(0_+)=u_C(0_-)$, $i_L(0_+)=i_L(0_-)$); $i, i_1, i_2, u_L$ can jump abruptly.
Worked Ex 2.2-3 (Page 97)

Switch closed at $t=0$:

Only $u_C$ and $i_L$ remain continuous at $t=0_+$; branch currents $i, i_1, i_2, i_3$ and inductor voltage $u_L$ jump abruptly.

Three-Element Method & First-Order ODE Derivations

UNIVERSAL THREE-ELEMENT FORMULA (VALID FOR ANY VOLTAGE OR CURRENT $f(t)$, $t \ge 0_+$)
$$f(t) = f(\infty) + \left[f(0_+) - f(\infty)\right] e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}} \quad (t \ge 0_+)$$
1. Initial Value $f(0_+)$

Find $u_C(0_-)$ or $i_L(0_-)$ at $t=0_-$. By Switching Law, $u_C(0_+)=u_C(0_-)$ and $i_L(0_+)=i_L(0_-)$. Replace $C$ with a voltage source $u_C(0_+)$ (or $L$ with a current source $i_L(0_+)$) in the $t=0_+$ circuit to solve for any desired $f(0_+)$.

2. Steady-State Value $f(\infty)$

Evaluate the post-switching circuit as $t \to \infty$ (new DC steady state): treat capacitor $C$ as an open circuit and inductor $L$ as a short circuit.

3. Time Constant $\tau$ (seconds)

Find the Thévenin equivalent resistance $R_0$ looking into the terminals of $C$ or $L$ in the post-switching passive network (with all independent sources deactivated):

• RC Circuit: $\tau = R_0 C$
• RL Circuit: $\tau = \dfrac{L}{R_0}$
RC CHARGING ODE DERIVATION (PAGE 99)
$U = R i + u_C = RC \dfrac{du_C}{dt} + u_C$
With $u_C(0_+) = U_0$, $u_C(\infty) = U$, $\tau = RC$:
$$u_C(t) = U + (U_0 - U) e^{-\frac{t}{RC}}$$
RL ENERGIZING ODE DERIVATION (PAGE 100)
$U = R i_L + u_L = R i_L + L \dfrac{di_L}{dt}$
With $i_L(0_+) = I_0$, $i_L(\infty) = \dfrac{U}{R}$, $\tau = \dfrac{L}{R}$:
$$i_L(t) = \frac{U}{R} + \left(I_0 - \frac{U}{R}\right) e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}$$
INTERACTIVE TRANSIENT PLOTTER

First-Order Response $f(t) = f(\infty) + [f(0_+) - f(\infty)]e^{-t/\tau}$

$f(t) = 10 - 10 e^{-t / 1.0}$

At $t = 3\tau$: 9.50 (95.0% step)
At $t = 5\tau$: 9.93 (99.3% steady)
